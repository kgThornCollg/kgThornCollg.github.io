personal essay - draft 5 - (duel)



I started animating at the start of 8th grade. The tedious practice quickly captured my imagination, and filled me with all sorts of wonder-- dreaming about the worlds I could manifest. One day, in 3D art class, I had a particularly motivating idea. At that point, I had only tackled small projects-- simple exercises, nothing particularly polished. My hunger for challenge and enthusiasm for art spilled into something much greater. I made a promise-- Commit everything to this cartoon, and finish it.


The project would be fairly simple, consisting of two robots. One would exercise patience, defensive strategy, and brute strength to knock their opponent off guard. The other attempts to use precision, swift movement, and offense to its advantage. Both would duel to the death.


During this one storm, I got to work animating. However, the turbulent air frequently knocked out the power, erasing the hours of progress I had made that day. Instead of stopping and waiting for the storm to end, I did something else. I took a breath, and started again. Power went out again. I started again. Power went out a third time. I started a third time. On and on the duel went between me and entropy. Nobody really won. Due to the repeated data loss, the net progress I made was very little. It likely wouldnt have made a difference if I had just given up and started working after the storm. But I didn't care. I had to keep going.


This "overfocus", or drive to "keep going", is a scary sensation. It helps orient me on my missions and priorities, but at the same time, I get carried away. The enthusiasm spills over into a masochistic glee in which I find myself meaninglessly fighting a storm.


Near the end of the project, I pushed myself to an extreme degree. I would wake up at 4 or 5 AM, and work for pretty much the entire day. If I didn't work hard enough, I would deny myself food, water, music, or even something as simple as a nice memory. It was a harrowing experience.


Sometimes determination less feels like me and more an external drive, materializing from somewhere else in my conscious. If I am particuarly "lazy", a bell rings, demanding I get out of bed. But if I listen to it too much, hell rings, overriding my more... self-preserving faculties.


I believe WHY we work affects our choices and faculties. In my head, "working hard" feels like two parallel ideals. One is the determination to achieve your dreams and hold true to the vision of your younger self. The other is scary for me to define, so I'll just let it speak for itself: "I am a nihilistic fury that screams out at my own inability to make a difference. 'I want to change things. I want them to be better.' But no matter how hard we try. Nothing changes. I think I like it this way. The meaninglessness of it all. At least this way I can pull joy out of suffering-- out of the nothingness that is achieved." Woah. Maybe chill out a bit?


Being alive can often feel like banging your head against a brick wall. Nothing really goes anywhere, and you're left feeling empty and confused. One might bring up the value of relationships, of deep connections, but I'm not a deep connections guy. Maybe it's just my age or nature, but I've always been more of a "big picture" person. It's why I love space and philosophy.


Much of the way I percieve the world has been affected by my father; He grew up in a Indian village, burdened with farm work and an unsavory upbringing. Words cant capture the awe I feel every time I think about his story-- the sheer effort and emotion that must have gone into that undertaking pushes me. From his humble beginnings he reached a PhD education, a nice job, steady living in America, and a family. He's part of the reason I believe in "hard-work" so much.


This cartoon ended up bringing to attention the more nihilistic mindset I've been adopting while I work. Since then, I've been less aggressive with my workflow and generally more hopeful about things. But sometimes I still slip into unsavory choices. While I think it would be just rosy to fight for the hopes and dreams of a younger me, my better instincts tell me that balance between the two is a better path to a healthier and sustainable future. Without overfocus, I wouldn't have been able to learn and experience as much as I have. Without a dream, there would be nothing to guide me, no goals to begin with, and no memories keeping my behaviours in check.


Two years later, the project was finished. It was a turbulent process, filled with setbacks and roadblocks. I spent 6 months without a clear structure, then scrapped what was incoherently there. Then I worked on a storyboard, key-poses, animation, cleanup, coloring, shading, and finally, the finishing touches. Each part took multiple months.


I didnt recieve much of a reward upon completion. I released it online to a couple thousand views (which wasn't much relative to the effort), and what felt like loose recognition from my peers. But I don't care. The process of creation is the most valuable part. Throw me back in time, and I would do it all over again. Except this time I work harder.
